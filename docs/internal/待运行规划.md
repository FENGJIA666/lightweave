# LightWeave planning preparation

Prepared from the official Devpost `plan-lens` template. The user delegated concept and production decisions to the manager team. The decisions below are manager decisions, not invented interview quotations. The official planning workflow will produce `devpost/design-brief.md` after Lens MCP and CLAD readiness are observed in the coding-agent session.

## The one spatial moment

The player shapes a golden beam through a mirror in a small garden. Each flower receives the light, opens into color, and adds its own musical note. Three blooms light up the entire garden.

Signature line: **Shape the light. Watch your world bloom.**

## Why it belongs on glasses

- Draws in the world: mirrors, light paths, and flowers occupy one shared three-dimensional work area.
- Leaves the body free: the player can change viewpoint and reach toward a large mirror control while watching the beam.
- The spatial relationship remains visible during interaction. A higher flower requires a change in elevation, giving the scene genuine depth.

## Product or overlay

- Type: product; the virtual optical garden is the experience.
- Sensing required: standard interaction input and the current viewpoint.
- Recognition stand-in: none needed.
- Runtime logic: ray intersections and reflection determine which flower receives the beam. Rotation buttons alter yaw or pitch and never directly set a flower to complete.

## Where things live

- Main work area: approximately 70 cm wide, centered 120 cm in front of the starting viewpoint and slightly below eye level (manager layout decision; finalize from preview).
- Emitter: front-center of the work area, with a gold source glow; use the optical library's workbench-local placement consistently.
- Mirror: in the center, at a reachable height; one mirror first, with a second mirror only after the complete core works.
- Three flower receivers: around the mirror in the same garden; at least one is visibly higher than the others.
- Task label: compact, bright, above the current receiver rather than a large face-fixed panel.
- Controls: large bright targets beside the mirror, with a reset/replay control beside the garden's status.

## Hands and input

- Pinch a large control to adjust yaw or pitch by a visible increment.
- Use standard grab/drag to manipulate the mirror after the repeatable button path is proven.
- Targets highlight when aimed at; a short sound confirms an accepted input.
- Replay clears the three bloom states and returns mirror orientation to the initial pose.
- Voice is not part of the interaction design.

## When things show

- Start cue: introduces the first beam interaction, then recedes once the player acts.
- Direction cue: appears near the active receiver; it yields to the bloom animation after a hit.
- Progress: three small flower marks close to the work area indicate the achieved blooms.
- Completion: after all three flowers bloom, the garden produces a bright celebration and shows **Garden Complete** plus **Replay**.
- Spatial sound points attention toward a blooming flower. Keep the full garden within the intended viewing area whenever practical.

## Done criteria

Each criterion must be checked in the actual Lens Studio Interactive Preview and mapped to a LEAF scenario.

1. At start, the emitter, mirror, and three unbloomed flowers are visible at the specified scale, with clear first-action guidance.
2. Pinching yaw/pitch controls changes mirror orientation and updates a continuous, geometrically reflected beam in the same frame sequence.
3. A real beam intersection with a receiver causes exactly that flower to bloom and updates progress once; a miss does not advance progress.
4. The elevated receiver can be reached through a non-horizontal reflected path, and the player can inspect that path from another viewpoint.
5. Three achieved receivers trigger Garden Complete; Replay returns the mirror, progress, and flowers to their initial states, allowing a complete second run.

## Out of scope for this build

- Live camera recognition, shared sessions, persistent anchors, and custom two-hand gestures.
- Account-bound cloud content during gameplay.
- A second mirror until the complete one-mirror garden works and is captured in preview.

## Risks and open questions

- Final control reach, text sizing, and edge framing require the first real runtime capture.
- Real-device comfort and hand tracking require later hardware observation; contest evidence uses Lens Studio preview.
- One-sided mirror behavior must be explained visually by a clearly different rear surface, or use an explicitly two-sided optical model consistently.
- The default miss path should end within the intended work area instead of stretching across the room. Check the initial view before recording.

## Build notes for CLAD
- Build for SPECS so it runs and can be demonstrated in Lens Studio's preview. No SPECS device is available.
- Use Spectacles Interaction Kit (SIK) and UI Kit for all interactions, so they work with the mouse in Interactive Preview and with LEAF.
- Build any stand-in exactly as described above, and keep it obvious in the scene.
- Use real-world scale in centimeters. Keep content inside the user's view during the main task.
- The display is additive: black renders as transparent. Don't rely on black or very dark content to be seen; keep text light and readable from where the user stands.
- Get the basic flow working first, then add detail.
- Write LEAF scenarios for each done criterion, run them in preview, and report which pass. Say which criteria have no test.
- Don't record, identify, or follow people.

## After the build: judge it
This part is for you, after CLAD finishes.

1. **Does it work?** Ask your agent which LEAF tests passed and which done criteria have no test.
2. **Walk around it.** Open the preview in Interactive Preview. Use WASD to move, Q and E to go down and up, and right-drag (or hold Shift) to look around. Clicking acts as a pinch. Stand where a user would and look at the task.
3. **Ask the three questions.**
   - Does it use what I'm looking at?
   - Does it put something in my space, where it belongs?
   - Does it free my hands or let me keep my eyes up?
   - Would it be just as good on a phone? If so, what would make it need the glasses?
4. **Check comfort.**
   - Everything I need is in view while I do the task. Nothing sits at the edges, behind me, or stuck to my face.
   - Every control is within reach of someone standing there.
   - Text reads easily from where I stand. No dark UI disappearing.
   - Things show when needed and get out of the way.
5. **Label what you know.** Write "logic proven" next to anything the tests and preview show working, and "feel unproven" next to anything only real glasses could tell you (comfort over time, real hand tracking, real recognition).
6. **Fix with design notes, not code.** Tell your agent what to change in design terms, for example: "Move the step card 15 cm lower and next to the pan. Make the Next button twice as big. Re-run the tests." If a fix takes under a minute, like nudging a panel, do it yourself in the scene.
7. **Submit.** Record a video under 3 minutes of your Lens in the preview, public on YouTube. Make your repo public with your Lens Studio project and this `devpost/` folder. Details are on the hackathon site: https://learn-specs.devpost.com/resources
